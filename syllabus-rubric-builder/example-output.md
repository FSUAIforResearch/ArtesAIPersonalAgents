### Course Description

This course introduces you to the core practices of data analysis in the social sciences. You will learn to translate social questions into measurable variables, describe and visualize data, reason about samples and uncertainty, conduct confidence-interval and hypothesis-test analyses, and fit and interpret regression models in R. The course emphasizes both technical practice and responsible communication: you will explain statistical results for non-expert audiences, create clear charts, identify limitations, and consider ethical and privacy issues when working with social-science data.

### Thematic Arc

1. **From social questions to data:** Questions, variables, measurement, surveys, sampling, and probability.
2. **From data to evidence:** Description, confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, group comparisons, and regression.
3. **From analysis to public communication:** Multiple regression, effective charts, interpretation for non-experts, ethics, privacy, and limitations.

### Prerequisites and Audience

This course is designed for sophomores and juniors. No prior statistics background is assumed. `[Instructor to fill: confirm any formal prerequisite or co-requisite, if applicable.]`

## 2. Measurable Course Learning Objectives

By the end of this course, you will be able to:

1. **Formulate** answerable social-science questions and identify the variables, populations, samples, and measurements needed to investigate them.
2. **Construct** reproducible R/RStudio or Posit Cloud workflows that import, inspect, transform, summarize, and visualize social-science data.
3. **Analyze** distributions and relationships using appropriate numerical summaries, plots, confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, group comparisons, and regression models.
4. **Evaluate** statistical evidence by interpreting estimates, uncertainty, model results, assumptions, and limitations in relation to a social-science question.
5. **Fit and interpret** simple and multiple regression models in R, including coefficients, uncertainty, controls, and practical meaning.
6. **Communicate** statistical findings to non-expert audiences through accurate prose, accessible charts, and clearly labeled tables or figures.
7. **Assess** ethical, privacy, sampling, measurement, and communication risks in social-science data analysis and propose responsible safeguards.

# Companion Skill 3: Final Project Report Rubric (`/rubric`)

**Assignment:** Final Project Report  
**Team:** 3 students  
**Length:** 6–8 pages  
**Assignment value:** 20% of the course grade  
**Purpose:** Analyze a public dataset in R, answer a social-science question, interpret results for a non-expert reader, and discuss limitations.

> The rubric below allocates 100 report points, which correspond to the report’s 20% course weight.

| Criterion (weight) | Exemplary | Proficient | Developing | Beginning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Question, data, and measurement (20%)** | States a focused, answerable social-science question; clearly defines the population, sample, variables, measurements, and dataset provenance. **18–20** | States a workable question and identifies most key variables and data details, with minor gaps. **16–17** | Question or measurement choices are broad, unclear, or incompletely justified. **12–15** | Question, data source, or variables are missing, mismatched, or not interpretable. **0–11** |
| **R analysis and reproducibility (25%)** | Uses appropriate, well-organized R code; analysis is reproducible; summaries, visualizations, and models are accurate and clearly connected to the question. **23–25** | R analysis is generally appropriate and accurate, with small coding, organization, or reproducibility issues. **20–22** | Analysis is partly appropriate but contains substantive errors, missing steps, or limited reproducibility. **15–19** | Analysis is absent, seriously inaccurate, or not connected to the reported results. **0–14** |
| **Statistical reasoning and interpretation (25%)** | Correctly interprets estimates, uncertainty, tests, group comparisons, and/or regression results in context; distinguishes statistical evidence from causal claims. **23–25** | Interpretation is mostly correct and contextualized, with limited overstatement or imprecision. **20–22** | Interpretation includes important errors, unexplained statistical terms, or unsupported claims. **15–19** | Results are misinterpreted, omitted, or presented as claims without statistical support. **0–14** |
| **Communication for a non-expert audience (15%)** | Presents a coherent argument with accurate plain-language prose, readable charts/tables, informative labels, and effective organization. **14–15** | Communication is clear overall, with minor issues in organization, accessibility, labeling, or jargon. **12–13** | Reader must work to follow the argument; charts, labels, or explanations are uneven. **9–11** | Report is difficult to follow, inaccessible, or missing key communication elements. **0–8** |
| **Limitations, ethics, and privacy (15%)** | Identifies meaningful sampling, measurement, model, generalizability, ethical, and privacy limitations and proposes responsible safeguards. **14–15** | Identifies several relevant limitations and ethical issues, with generally reasonable discussion. **12–13** | Mentions limitations or ethics superficially, with little connection to the study. **9–11** | Omits or seriously misunderstands limitations, ethics, or privacy responsibilities. **0–8** |

**Rubric total: 100 report points = 20% of the course grade.**

### Learning Objective Alignment

- R analysis and reproducibility → Objective 2.
- Statistical reasoning and interpretation → Objectives 3, 4, and 5.
- Communication for a non-expert audience → Objective 6.
